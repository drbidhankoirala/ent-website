Oral cancer is one of the most common cancers in South Asia, yet it is also one of the most preventable and — when caught early — one of the most curable. The problem is that most patients come to me at a late stage, when the cancer has already spread to the neck or beyond. This happens because the early warning signs are easy to confuse with ordinary mouth problems.

This article explains what to look for and when to take action.

What Is Oral Cancer?

Oral cancer refers to cancer that develops anywhere inside the mouth — on the tongue, cheeks, lips, gums, the floor of the mouth, or the roof of the mouth (palate). It usually begins as a small, painless change that is easy to ignore.

Early Warning Signs

See a doctor immediately if you notice any of the following for more than 3 weeks:

  • A mouth ulcer (ghau) that does not heal
  • A white patch (leukoplakia) or red patch (erythroplakia) inside the mouth
  • A lump or thickening inside the cheek, tongue, or lips
  • Difficulty chewing, swallowing, or moving your jaw or tongue
  • Unexplained bleeding from the mouth
  • Numbness or pain in any area of the mouth or lips
  • A feeling that something is stuck in your throat
  • A lump or swelling in the neck
  • Loosening of teeth without any dental cause
⚠ Important:

A painless ulcer or patch is more suspicious than a painful one. Pain is not a reliable indicator of cancer. Many early oral cancers are completely painless — do not wait for pain before seeing a doctor.

Who Is at Risk?

In Nepal, oral cancer is strongly linked to tobacco and betel nut (paan, supari, khaini) use. If you regularly use any of these, your risk is significantly higher. Other risk factors include:

  • Smoking (cigarettes, bidis, hookah)
  • Smokeless tobacco (khaini, naswar, gutka)
  • Betel nut (supari, paan masala)
  • Heavy alcohol use
  • Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection
  • Poor oral hygiene and ill-fitting dentures causing repeated trauma

Why Early Detection Matters

Oral cancer detected at Stage I or II has a cure rate of over 80%. By Stage III or IV — when the cancer has reached the neck — that rate drops significantly, and treatment becomes much more complex, involving larger surgeries, radiation, and sometimes chemotherapy.

A simple clinical examination takes less than 5 minutes. If something looks suspicious, a biopsy provides a definitive answer. This is not something to delay.

What to Do Next

If you or a family member has any of the signs listed above lasting more than 3 weeks, book a consultation. Do not wait and watch. Early-stage oral cancer is treatable with surgery alone — catching it late changes everything.
